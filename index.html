Period guide

Simple information to help you understand and prepare for your cycle.

Cycle basics

🗓️ How long is a normal cycle?

Your cycle runs from the first day of one period to the day before the next one. For most adults it is between 21 and 35 days, and the period itself usually lasts 2 to 7 days. It is normal for your cycle to change by a few days from month to month.

🔄 The four phases

Period: the lining of the uterus sheds. Follicular: your body prepares an egg. Ovulation: an egg is released, usually about 14 days before your next period. Luteal: hormones rise and then drop, and PMS symptoms can show up.

🌀 Irregular cycles

Irregular periods are common in the first few years after periods begin, after pregnancy, around perimenopause, and during stress, illness, or big changes in weight or exercise. Tracking helps you see your own pattern.

📈 Why track?

Logging symptoms and moods for a few cycles helps you see which days are harder, plan ahead, and give a doctor clear information if you ever need it.

What might I feel?

🩸 During your period

Bleeding, cramps, tiredness, headaches, backache and changes in mood can happen. Symptoms vary from person to person and from cycle to cycle.

🌱 After your period

Energy and mood may improve for some people. Discharge and energy can change throughout the cycle.

🌼 Around ovulation

Some people notice slippery cervical mucus or mild one-sided discomfort. Ovulation dates are estimates, not guarantees.

🌙 Before your period

Bloating, tender breasts, cravings, fatigue, irritability or feeling emotional can occur in the days before bleeding begins.

What should I keep handy?

Period products

Pads, tampons, period underwear or a menstrual cup — whichever you're comfortable using.

Comfort

A heating pad or warm water bottle, comfortable clothes and rest when you need it.

Everyday essentials

Water, regular meals, spare underwear and any usual pain relief you can safely take.

Easing cramps and low days

🔥 Warmth

A heating pad, warm water bottle or warm bath on your lower belly or back can ease cramps.

🚶 Gentle movement

Walking, stretching or yoga helps some people with pain, bloating and mood.

💊 Pain relief

Over-the-counter pain relievers such as ibuprofen help many people. Follow the label, and ask a pharmacist or doctor if you are not sure it is safe for you.

🥗 Food and water

Regular meals and plenty of water help. If your periods are heavy, iron-rich foods such as beans, leafy greens and red meat can help. Less caffeine and salty food may ease bloating and tension for some people.

😴 Sleep and rest

A steady sleep routine can help your mood and energy in the days before your period. Rest when you need it.

Using period products safely

Tampons

Change them at least every 4 to 8 hours and use the lowest absorbency that works for your flow. Do not leave one in longer than 8 hours, including overnight. A pad can be a good choice for sleeping.

Pads, cups and period underwear

Change pads every few hours. Menstrual cups and period underwear have their own wear times, so follow the instructions that come with them.

Toxic shock syndrome (rare but serious)

Sudden high fever, a sunburn-like rash, vomiting, diarrhea, dizziness or muscle aches during or just after your period need urgent medical care. Remove any tampon and get help right away.

When to get medical help

Get urgent care for severe or sudden pelvic pain, fainting, a fever with pain, or bleeding that soaks through a pad or tampon every hour for two or more hours, especially with dizziness or shortness of breath.

Book a visit if you notice any of these:

Periods lasting longer than 7 days, or passing clots larger than a quarter

Cycles shorter than 21 days or longer than 35 days

Missing three periods in a row (take a pregnancy test first if it is possible)

Pain that stops you from your usual activities

Bleeding between periods or after sex

Feeling very tired, dizzy or out of breath, which can be a sign of low iron

Mood changes that seriously affect your work, relationships or safety

PMDD

PMDD: premenstrual dysphoric disorder

PMDD is a real medical condition, not "just bad PMS" and not overreacting. It affects roughly 3 to 8 percent of people who menstruate.

What it is

PMDD is a severe form of premenstrual symptoms that disrupts daily life. It is not caused by abnormal hormone levels. Researchers believe some people's brains are extra sensitive to the normal hormone changes in the luteal phase.

What it can feel like

Intense irritability or anger, sadness or hopelessness, anxiety or feeling on edge, sudden mood swings, and feeling overwhelmed or out of control. Many people also have trouble concentrating, tiredness, appetite or sleep changes, breast tenderness, bloating, and aches.

The pattern is the clue

Symptoms start in the week or two before your period, ease within a few days after bleeding begins, and mostly disappear the week after. When this repeats cycle after cycle, it points to PMDD rather than general stress or depression.

How it is diagnosed

There is no blood test. A doctor usually asks you to track symptoms daily for at least two cycles. Use the Symptoms and Journal tabs every day, then show your doctor the pattern.

Treatment options to ask about

Antidepressants (SSRIs) taken daily or only in the second half of the cycle, certain birth control pills, talking therapy such as CBT, regular exercise, steady sleep, and less caffeine and alcohol. Different things work for different people, so it can take some trial and error.

🗒️ Build a daily tracking habit

Use the Symptoms and Journal tabs every day. Write down the day your symptoms start and the day they ease. After two or three cycles you will see your pattern clearly, which helps you plan and gives a doctor real information.

🧭 Make a plan while you feel well

In the week after your period, when you feel most like yourself, write a short plan: what helps you, who to tell, and what to avoid. Look at the droplets on your wheel so you know when your luteal phase begins, and open your plan then.

🍽️ Steady your body

Eat regular meals with protein and slow-release carbs like oats, brown rice or beans, so your blood sugar and mood stay steadier. Cut back on caffeine and alcohol in your luteal phase, since they can make anxiety and mood swings worse. Keep a regular sleep schedule, even when sleep feels hard.

🏃 Move every day

Regular exercise is one of the most helpful habits for mood. Even a 20 minute walk counts. On heavy days, gentle stretching or yoga is enough.

🌬️ Calming tools for hard moments

Try slow breathing (in for 4, out for 6, a few times). Try grounding: name 5 things you see, 4 you can touch, 3 you hear. Step outside, drink some water, or write down what you are feeling. Reminding yourself "this is a pattern and it usually eases once my period starts" can make hard days easier, and your feelings are still real.

🗣️ Tell the people close to you

Let your trusted person know when your luteal phase starts. Agree on a simple signal for needing a pause or space. Try to save big decisions and hard conversations for after your period begins, when you feel more like yourself.

🧺 Lighten your load

Where you can, plan fewer commitments in the week before your period. Prepare easy meals, ask for help with chores, and give yourself permission to rest.

🩺 Get the most from a doctor visit

Bring your tracking record, a list of your symptoms, how they affect your work and relationships, and what you have tried. Ask about PMDD directly. If you feel dismissed, you can ask for a second opinion or a referral to someone familiar with PMDD. In Canada, a family doctor or walk-in clinic is a good place to start.

🤝 If you are supporting someone with PMDD

Listen without saying "it's just hormones" or "calm down." Offer something specific, like making dinner or taking over a task. Check in gently, and do not take hard moments personally. Help them follow their plan, and know the crisis supports below.

If you feel unsafe

PMDD can bring dark thoughts or deep hopelessness. If you ever think about harming yourself, call or text 9-8-8 (Canada's Suicide Crisis Helpline, free, 24/7, in English and French). If you are in immediate danger, call 911. Tell someone you trust. The International Association for Premenstrual Disorders (iapmd.org) offers information and peer support.

This guide is general information, not medical advice. A healthcare professional can help you decide what is right for you.
